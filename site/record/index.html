Did our flagged price hold to post?

Our flags are straight win bets. In a pari-mutuel pool your payout is set by the final odds no matter when you bet, so there is no price to hold you to . This table is a model diagnostic, not a bet rule. Overlay at close counts the flags whose closing odds still beat our fair number: the value we saw was real at post. Value at the close is the stricter test. the close also reached the reference price we recorded before the race. When these run low, it means our fair line is too long, usually because it is anchored to a generous morning line rather than the real market. That is the known v0 weakness the version notes track. ML→close is the opposite signal: how far the crowd moved toward our flags after we published.

ML→close = to-1 points the price moved from the morning line to post. Positive means it shortened. This is descriptive only: because your payout is the final price regardless of when you bet, our “play at” figure is a reference price and not a rule. There is no earlier price to catch and nothing to hold you to. To be unambiguous, because it matters: a flag that closes below that figure is still graded on the public record, win or lose. It does not become a pass after the fact. A run of “short” closes is a sign our number was set against a loose morning line, not the real market.

Our call is an early hold/shorten read set from each flag’s committed board price. Long-priced flags the crowd tends to leave alone hold, short ones get bet down. It is a heuristic on a small sample, shown next to what actually happened so you can watch it prove out or fail in public. Not a bet-timing tip: you are paid the closing price whenever you bet, so a flag we call “likely bet down” is simply a likely pass.

Brier score: lower is better (0 = perfect). Ours and the morning line’s sit within a thousandth of each other, and that is the honest headline: a standalone Brier comparison cannot tell a real model from a plausible one. Both numbers are dominated by the fact that most horses lose, and a model that did nothing but copy the board would score the same as the board.

So we ask the question that separates them: does our number add anything the board does not already contain? Fit the outcome on the market’s estimate alone, then again with ours added, and test whether the second fit is genuinely better.

The answer is suggestive, at the edge of significance, on a sample too small to lean on. We are publishing all of it rather than the flattering part.

The test was chosen and written down before we ran it, by someone other than the person who built the model. That matters more than the result. Anyone can fit models until one of them clears a threshold and then publish that one; the only defence is to fix the method in advance and be bound by whatever it returns. The estimator below, a conditional logit on race choice sets, was specified, written and frozen by a separate reviewer before any of these numbers existed, and we are reporting exactly what it produced, including the two checks that go against us.

Frozen analysis, as of 2026-07-31. Every figure in this section is pinned to the ledger as it stood that day and is deliberately not re-fit as new races are graded. Re-fitting a pre-registered test to newer data is the thing pre-registration exists to prevent. The race and runner counts below are therefore smaller than the calibration counts above, and that is expected, not an error. When it is re-run, it will be re-run in full and dated again.

The pre-registered test is a conditional logit over race choice sets, 138 races, 1,170 runners. Our estimate carries a coefficient of +0.6643 (standard error 0.3013) once the board is already in the model, likelihood-ratio χ²(1) = 4.833, p = 0.0279.

Two robustness checks disagree with that, and they matter more than the headline. Allowing for the fact that horses in the same race are not independent gives a cluster-robust p of 0.0548, the wrong side of the conventional 0.05 line. And when whole races are resampled and the test refit, 41% of those refits fail to reach significance at all. A result that disappears two times in five under resampling is not a finding to build on.

An earlier version of this page reported p = 0.026 from the same data. That number was overstated and we are correcting it. It treated all 1,181 runners as independent observations. They are not: exactly one horse wins each race, and the probabilities are worked out relative to the others in that race, so the real number of independent units is the 138 races, not the runners. Ignoring that makes any result look stronger than it is.

We are deliberately not printing a yes or no here. The method and every number are published together so you can weigh them yourself, and the full output is in the machine-readable record. Judge it again at the end of the meet, when there is more of it.
